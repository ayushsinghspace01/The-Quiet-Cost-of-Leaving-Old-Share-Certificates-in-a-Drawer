# The-Quiet-Cost-of-Leaving-Old-Share-Certificates-in-a-Drawer
Nobody decides to leave share certificates unconverted. It just happens.

The paperwork looks tedious. The company is one nobody has followed in years. There is no deadline, no reminder, and no obvious penalty for waiting. So the folder goes back in the almirah and the intention to deal with it moves to next year.

The problem is that the situation is not static. Several clocks are running in the background, and each year of delay makes the eventual process harder and, in some cases, more expensive. Understanding what those clocks are is usually enough to move the task up the list.
Clock one: the seven-year dividend rule
This is the one that catches most families, and it operates silently.

Under the Companies Act, if dividends on a holding remain unclaimed for seven consecutive years, the underlying shares themselves are transferred to the Investor Education and Protection Fund. Not the dividends alone. The shares.

The holding is still yours. It has not been confiscated. But recovering it now runs through a separate process involving Form IEPF-5, the company's nodal officer, and the IEPF Authority, which adds months to whatever you were originally trying to do.

The trigger is almost always mundane. A house move, an address never updated with the registrar, dividend warrants posted to a place the family no longer lived, and nobody noticing because nobody was expecting them. Seven years of that and the transfer happens automatically.

If your certificates date from the 1990s and there has been a change of address in the family since, check the IEPF portal before assuming the shares are sitting where the certificate suggests.
Clock two: succession gets more complicated every generation
A holding in one living person's name, with a registered nominee, is a simple case. Everything else gets progressively harder.

Consider how it compounds. The original holder dies without a nomination and without a will. The shares pass to a spouse and three children as legal heirs, but nobody completes the transmission because the amount seemed small at the time. Twenty years later the spouse has also died, one child has died leaving their own heirs, and one has moved abroad.

What was a single transmission with one death certificate is now a chain of transmissions requiring documentation for each link, no-objection declarations from a widening circle of heirs, and coordination across people who may not be in regular contact or in the same country.

SEBI has genuinely simplified transmission for smaller holdings. Where the value of physical securities per listed company sits below a specified threshold, the registrar can accept an affidavit, an indemnity bond, and no-objection letters from other heirs in place of a court-issued succession certificate. The threshold is revised periodically, so confirm the current figure with the registrar.

But simplification applies to the process, not the arithmetic. Two heirs is easier than eleven, and the number only moves in one direction.
Clock three: records degrade and memory disappears
Registrars change. Companies merge, rename, and demerge. The person who knew which certificates related to which folio, and who remembered that the shares were bought through a broker in Fort in 1987, does not live forever.

Practical things that get harder with time:

Establishing that "R. K. Mehta" on the certificate and "Rajesh Kumar Mehta" on a PAN card are the same person becomes harder once the person who could swear to it is gone.
Tracing a company through three mergers is easier when someone remembers the original business.
Locating missing certificates from a set is far easier while the household filing system is intact.
Signature verification is straightforward while the holder is alive and can attend a bank.

That last point deserves emphasis. A living holder can walk into their bank, have a signature attested on Form ISR-2, and resolve in an afternoon what becomes a documentation exercise after their death.

If an elderly family member holds physical certificates, this is the single strongest argument for acting now rather than later.
Clock four: you cannot sell what you have not converted
Since April 2019, SEBI has not permitted transfer of listed securities in physical form. A paper certificate cannot be sold, pledged, or handed to a buyer. Transmission and transposition remain available in physical form, but any actual sale requires the holding to sit in a demat account first.

This matters most at exactly the wrong moment. Families typically discover the restriction when they need the money, and then find themselves waiting out a conversion process that could have been completed at leisure years earlier.

The mechanics are not difficult once the folio is in order. Certificates are defaced, submitted with a Dematerialization Request Form through a depository participant, forwarded to the registrar, verified against the register, and credited. Clean cases move in weeks. This walkthrough on how to convert physical shares into demat sets out the sequence and the forms involved at each stage.

The variable is never the process. It is whether your documents match the register, which is a problem best solved without a deadline pressing on it.
What happens when you finally sell
Worth knowing in advance, because it changes how you should prepare.

Cost of acquisition is the usual headache. For shares bought decades ago, the original purchase price is often unrecoverable. No contract note, no broker, no bank statement from 1989.

For listed equity, the grandfathering provision introduced in 2018 helps considerably. For shares acquired before 1 February 2018, the cost of acquisition is taken as the higher of the actual cost and the lower of the fair market value as on 31 January 2018 and the sale consideration. In practice this means the 31 January 2018 market price often becomes your effective cost base, which both simplifies the computation and reduces the taxable gain relative to a 1980s purchase price.

The exchanges published closing prices for that date, and they remain accessible.

Rates changed in 2024. Long term capital gains on listed equity and the annual exemption threshold were both revised in the 2024 budget. Since these figures move, confirm the current position with a tax adviser rather than relying on a number in an article.

Corporate actions have to be reconstructed. Thirty years of bonus issues, splits, rights issues, and mergers mean your holding today is probably not the quantity printed on the certificate. Each event has its own cost allocation treatment. The registrar can usually supply a corporate action history for the folio, and asking for it during the conversion process is easier than asking for it two years later at the time of sale.

Delisted companies are a different question. Section 112A applies to listed equity with STT paid. Holdings in delisted or unlisted companies follow different rules.
A reasonable order of operations
If you are starting from a folder of certificates:

Identify the current entity. Companies rename and merge; find who trades today and who their registrar is.
Search the IEPF portal for the holder's name and the company.
Update the folio: PAN, KYC, bank details, nomination.
Resolve name and signature discrepancies while the holder is alive if at all possible.
Complete any pending transmission.
Convert.
Ask the registrar for the corporate action history and record the 31 January 2018 closing prices while you are at it.

Step seven takes ten minutes and saves an afternoon of reconstruction later.

The actual argument for doing it now
None of this is urgent in the sense of a deadline. That is precisely why it gets deferred.

But every year of waiting adds a little friction. Another year toward a seven-year IEPF threshold. Another year of the family tree branching. Another year of institutional and personal memory thinning out.

The conversion itself, done on a folio that is in order, is a few weeks of largely administrative work. Done on a folio that has been left for two decades, across a deceased holder and scattered heirs, it becomes a project.

The paperwork does not get easier. It only gets older.
